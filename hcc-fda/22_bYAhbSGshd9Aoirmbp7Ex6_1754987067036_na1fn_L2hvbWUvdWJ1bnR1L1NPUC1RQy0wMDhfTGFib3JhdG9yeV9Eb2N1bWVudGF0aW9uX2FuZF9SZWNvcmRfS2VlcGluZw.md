# Standard Operating Procedure

## SOP-QC-008: Laboratory Documentation and Record Keeping

**Version:** 1.0
**Effective Date:** 2025-12-08
**Category:** Quality Control
**Compliance Areas:** FDA, USDA, cGMP

## 1.0 Purpose and Scope

This Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) outlines the requirements and procedures for effective documentation and record keeping within the laboratory operations of large animal pharmaceutical manufacturing. The purpose of this SOP is to ensure that all laboratory activities, including but not limited to sample handling, testing, data analysis, and reporting, are accurately, legibly, contemporaneously, originally, and accurately (ALCOA) documented and maintained in compliance with applicable regulatory requirements, including those set forth by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA), and current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP). This SOP applies to all personnel involved in laboratory activities, documentation, and record management within the facility.

### 2.1 Laboratory Management

Laboratory Management is responsible for the overall implementation and adherence to this SOP. This includes providing adequate resources, ensuring personnel are properly trained, reviewing and approving documentation, and overseeing the maintenance of all laboratory records. They are also responsible for establishing and maintaining a robust quality management system that supports accurate and reliable documentation practices. This oversight ensures that all laboratory operations align with regulatory expectations and internal quality standards [1].

### 2.2 Laboratory Personnel

All laboratory personnel are responsible for accurately, legibly, contemporaneously, originally, and accurately (ALCOA) documenting all activities performed. This includes recording raw data, observations, calculations, and results in designated laboratory notebooks, forms, or electronic systems. Personnel must ensure that all entries are attributable to the individual performing the task and are made at the time the activity occurs. Any deviations from established procedures must be documented and justified. Adherence to these principles is crucial for maintaining data integrity and traceability [2].

### 2.3 Quality Assurance (QA) Department

The Quality Assurance (QA) Department is responsible for independently reviewing and auditing laboratory documentation and records to ensure compliance with this SOP, internal policies, and regulatory requirements. QA plays a critical role in identifying discrepancies, ensuring data integrity, and verifying that all documentation practices meet cGMP standards. They are also involved in the approval of new or revised documentation and in the investigation of any documentation-related non-conformances [3].

### 2.4 Document Control Personnel

Document Control Personnel are responsible for the management and control of all official laboratory documents, including SOPs, test methods, specifications, and raw data forms. Their responsibilities include issuing controlled copies, maintaining document revision histories, archiving retired documents, and ensuring that only current and approved versions of documents are in use. This function is vital for preventing the use of obsolete documents and for maintaining an organized and accessible documentation system [4].

### 3.1 General Documentation Practices

All laboratory documentation must adhere to Good Documentation Practices (GDP) to ensure data integrity, traceability, and accuracy. This includes ensuring that all entries are: **A**ttributable (who made the entry), **L**egible (easily readable), **C**ontemporaneous (recorded at the time of the activity), **O**riginal (first capture of information), and **A**ccurate (correct and truthful) [5].

*   **3.1.1 Use of Approved Forms and Templates:** All data must be recorded on approved, controlled forms, logbooks, or electronic systems. Unauthorized forms or loose papers are not permitted for official record-keeping. Forms must be pre-numbered or uniquely identified to ensure traceability.

*   **3.1.2 Penmanship and Legibility:** All handwritten entries must be made in indelible, black or blue ink. Entries must be legible and clear. Correction fluid or erasable pens are strictly prohibited. Any errors must be corrected by drawing a single line through the incorrect entry, writing the correct entry adjacent to it, initialing, and dating the change, along with a brief explanation for the correction [6].

*   **3.1.3 Contemporaneous Recording:** All data and observations must be recorded at the time the activity is performed. Backdating or postdating entries is strictly forbidden. If an entry cannot be made immediately, the reason for the delay must be documented, and the entry must reflect the actual time of the activity.

*   **3.1.4 Attributable Entries:** All entries must be clearly attributable to the individual who performed the action or made the observation. This includes initialing and dating each entry. For electronic systems, user login credentials serve as attribution.

*   **3.1.5 Original Records:** Original records must be maintained. If a copy is made for any reason, the original must be clearly identified and retained as per record retention policies. Electronic records must have audit trails that capture all changes, including the identity of the person making the change, the date, and the time [7].

### 3.2 Sample Management and Testing Documentation

*   **3.2.1 Sample Receipt and Login:** Upon receipt, all samples must be visually inspected for integrity and proper labeling. Each sample must be assigned a unique laboratory identification number, which is then recorded in a sample login logbook or electronic system. The logbook entry must include the date of receipt, source, sample type, quantity, and any relevant client or batch information. Any discrepancies must be documented and resolved before sample processing [8].

*   **3.2.2 Sample Preparation:** All steps involved in sample preparation, including weighing, dilution, and extraction, must be documented. This includes recording the weights of reagents, volumes of solvents, and any calculations performed. Equipment used, such as balances and pipettes, must be identified by their unique identification numbers.

*   **3.2.3 Analytical Testing:** For each analytical test performed, the following must be documented:
    *   **Test Method Reference:** The specific version of the test method (SOP) used.
    *   **Equipment Used:** Identification number of all major equipment used (e.g., HPLC, spectrophotometer, pH meter).
    *   **Reagents and Standards:** Lot numbers, expiry dates, and preparation details of all reagents, solvents, and reference standards.
    *   **Raw Data:** All raw data generated during the test, including chromatograms, spectra, instrument printouts, and manual readings. These must be clearly labeled with sample identification and date/time.
    *   **Calculations:** All calculations performed to derive the final results must be documented and verifiable. This includes intermediate calculations and conversion factors.
    *   **Results:** Final quantitative and qualitative results must be recorded, along with units of measure. Any out-of-specification (OOS) or out-of-trend (OOT) results must be clearly flagged and documented for further investigation [9].

*   **3.2.4 Quality Control (QC) Sample Documentation:** All QC samples (e.g., blanks, standards, controls, spikes) must be documented in the same manner as test samples. The results of QC samples must be reviewed against acceptance criteria, and any failures must be investigated and documented.

### 3.3 Data Review and Approval

*   **3.3.1 First-Level Review:** All laboratory data and documentation must undergo a first-level review by a qualified analyst other than the one who generated the data. This review ensures accuracy, completeness, and adherence to the test method and GDP. The reviewer must initial and date each page reviewed.

*   **3.3.2 Second-Level (Supervisory) Review:** A second-level review must be performed by a supervisor or designated qualified personnel. This review focuses on the overall integrity of the data, compliance with regulatory requirements, and the validity of the conclusions. The supervisor must sign and date the final report or relevant documentation, indicating approval of the data [10].

### 3.4 Archiving and Retention

*   **3.4.1 Record Retention Period:** All laboratory records, including raw data, analytical reports, logbooks, and instrument printouts, must be retained for a minimum of one year after the expiration date of the drug product or as specified by regulatory requirements, whichever is longer. For large animal pharmaceutical products, specific USDA regulations may also apply to retention periods [11].

*   **3.4.2 Storage Conditions:** Records must be stored in a secure environment that protects them from damage, loss, or unauthorized access. This includes protection from environmental factors such as extreme temperatures, humidity, and light. Electronic records must be backed up regularly and stored in a manner that ensures their integrity and retrievability throughout the retention period.

*   **3.4.3 Archival and Retrieval:** A systematic archival system must be in place to facilitate easy retrieval of records when needed for audits, inspections, or investigations. A detailed inventory of archived documents must be maintained.

### 4.1 Laboratory Notebooks

Laboratory notebooks, whether physical or electronic, are primary records for all experimental work. Each entry must be dated, signed by the experimenter, and countersigned by a supervisor. All raw data, observations, calculations, and deviations must be recorded directly into the notebook. Pages must be numbered sequentially, and no pages should be removed or left blank. For electronic notebooks, robust audit trails are essential to track all changes and user activities [12].

### 4.2 Analytical Data Sheets and Forms

Pre-approved analytical data sheets and forms must be used for recording specific test results. These forms should include fields for sample identification, test method, instrument used, raw data, calculated results, units, date of analysis, and analyst's signature. Any modifications to these forms must follow the change control procedure outlined in Section 9.0.

### 4.3 Instrument Logbooks

Dedicated logbooks must be maintained for all laboratory instruments. These logbooks should record instrument usage, calibration, maintenance, and any troubleshooting activities. Each entry must include the date, time, activity performed, and the initials of the person performing the activity. This ensures a complete history of instrument performance and compliance with calibration schedules [13].

### 4.4 Reagent and Standard Preparation Records

Detailed records of all reagent and standard preparations must be maintained. This includes the name of the substance, manufacturer, lot number, expiry date, date of preparation, concentration, solvent used, and the initials of the person who prepared it. For standards, the certificate of analysis and traceability to primary standards must be documented. These records are critical for ensuring the quality and accuracy of analytical results.

### 4.5 Training Records

Comprehensive training records must be maintained for all personnel. These records should include the date of training, the topics covered, the trainer's name, and the trainee's signature. Proficiency assessments and re-training schedules should also be documented. This ensures that all personnel are qualified to perform their assigned tasks and are aware of the latest SOP revisions [14].

### 4.6 Quality Control Records

All quality control data, including results from system suitability tests, calibration checks, and control charts, must be documented. Any out-of-specification (OOS) or out-of-trend (OOT) results must be thoroughly investigated, and the investigation findings, root cause, and corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) must be documented. These records demonstrate the ongoing control and performance of the laboratory's analytical systems.

### 4.7 Archival and Retrieval System

A well-defined system for archiving and retrieving all laboratory documentation must be in place. This system should include clear procedures for document indexing, storage location, retention periods, and destruction. Both physical and electronic archives must be secure and protected from unauthorized access, damage, or loss. Regular audits of the archival system should be conducted to ensure its effectiveness [15].

### 5.1 Initial Training

All new laboratory personnel must undergo comprehensive initial training on this SOP and all other relevant SOPs, test methods, and regulatory guidelines (FDA, USDA, cGMP). This training must cover general documentation practices, specific record-keeping procedures for their assigned tasks, and the importance of data integrity. Training should be conducted by qualified personnel and include both theoretical and practical components. Proficiency must be demonstrated before personnel are authorized to perform tasks independently [16].

### 5.2 Ongoing Training and Retraining

Ongoing training programs must be implemented to ensure that laboratory personnel remain proficient in documentation and record-keeping practices. This includes periodic retraining on this SOP, especially when significant revisions are made. Refresher training should also address common documentation errors or new regulatory expectations. Training effectiveness must be assessed regularly through quizzes, practical demonstrations, or performance reviews.

### 5.3 Training Documentation

All training activities must be thoroughly documented. Training records must include:
*   **Trainee Name and Employee ID:** Clear identification of the individual trained.
*   **Training Date:** The date(s) on which the training occurred.
*   **Training Topic/SOP ID:** Specific SOPs or topics covered during the training.
*   **Trainer Name and Signature:** Identification of the qualified individual who conducted the training.
*   **Trainee Signature:** Confirmation by the trainee that they received and understood the training.
*   **Assessment of Effectiveness:** Documentation of how the trainee's proficiency was assessed (e.g., passing score on a quiz, successful completion of a practical exercise).

These records are critical for demonstrating compliance with regulatory training requirements and for ensuring that only qualified personnel perform laboratory activities [17].

### 6.1 Internal Audits

Regular internal audits of laboratory documentation and record-keeping practices must be conducted by the Quality Assurance (QA) Department. These audits ensure ongoing compliance with this SOP, internal policies, and regulatory requirements. Audit findings, including any non-conformances or observations, must be documented, and corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) must be implemented and tracked to closure. The frequency of audits should be based on a risk assessment [18].

### 6.2 Management Review

Laboratory management must periodically review the effectiveness of the documentation and record-keeping system. This review should include an assessment of audit findings, deviations, CAPAs, and feedback from personnel. The management review ensures that the system remains suitable, adequate, and effective in meeting its objectives and regulatory expectations.

### 6.3 Data Integrity Checks

Automated and manual data integrity checks must be implemented to ensure the accuracy, completeness, and consistency of electronic and manual records. This includes:
*   **Audit Trail Review:** Regular review of electronic system audit trails to detect any unauthorized changes or unusual activities.
*   **Data Verification:** Periodic verification of raw data against calculated results and final reports.
*   **System Validation:** Validation of electronic systems used for data capture and storage to ensure their reliability and compliance with regulatory requirements [19].

### 6.4 Deviation and Non-Conformance Management

Any deviations from established documentation procedures or non-conformances related to record keeping must be documented, investigated, and resolved. The investigation should identify the root cause of the deviation, and appropriate CAPAs must be implemented to prevent recurrence. All deviation reports and CAPA records must be maintained as part of the quality system documentation.

---




### 7.1 Confidentiality and Data Security

All laboratory documentation and records, especially those containing sensitive or proprietary information, must be handled with strict confidentiality. Access to physical and electronic records must be restricted to authorized personnel only. Electronic systems must be protected by strong passwords, firewalls, and encryption where appropriate. Regular cybersecurity audits should be conducted to identify and mitigate potential vulnerabilities [20].

### 7.2 Physical Security of Records

Physical records must be stored in secure, access-controlled areas to prevent unauthorized access, theft, or damage. Fire suppression systems, environmental controls (temperature and humidity), and pest control measures must be in place to protect the integrity of the records. Off-site storage facilities, if used, must also meet these security and environmental standards.

### 7.3 Data Backup and Recovery

Regular backups of all electronic laboratory data and records must be performed. Backup copies should be stored off-site in a secure location to protect against data loss due to unforeseen events such as natural disasters or system failures. A robust data recovery plan must be in place and tested periodically to ensure that data can be restored quickly and accurately in the event of a system malfunction or disaster [21].

### 8.1 U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)

The FDA provides comprehensive regulations and guidance for pharmaceutical manufacturing, including specific requirements for laboratory documentation and record keeping. Key regulations include:
*   **21 CFR Part 211 - Current Good Manufacturing Practice for Finished Pharmaceuticals:** This part outlines the minimum cGMP requirements for the methods, facilities, and controls used in the manufacture, processing, packing, or holding of a drug product. Subpart J, "Records and Reports," specifically addresses laboratory records, distribution records, and complaint files [22].
*   **Guidance for Industry: cGMP for Phase 1 Investigational Drugs:** While focused on Phase 1, this guidance emphasizes the importance of accurate and complete documentation from early stages of drug development [23].
*   **Guidance for Industry: Data Integrity and Compliance With cGMP:** This guidance provides the FDA’s current thinking on data integrity, emphasizing the ALCOA principles and the need for robust systems to prevent unauthorized access, changes, or deletions of data [24].
*   **21 CFR Part 507 - Current Good Manufacturing Practice, Hazard Analysis, and Risk-Based Preventive Controls for Food for Animals:** While primarily for animal food, this regulation sets precedents for documentation and record-keeping practices in facilities handling animal-related products, which can be relevant for large animal pharmaceutical manufacturing [25].

### 8.2 U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA)

The USDA regulates veterinary biologics and certain aspects of animal health products. Relevant regulations and guidelines include:
*   **9 CFR Part 114 - Production Requirements for Biological Products:** This part outlines the requirements for the production of veterinary biological products, including aspects related to facility, equipment, and record keeping [26].
*   **USDA APHIS Veterinary Biologics Program:** This program provides guidance and regulations for the licensing, testing, and production of veterinary biologics, which often involve extensive laboratory documentation [27].

### 8.3 Current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP)

cGMP regulations are fundamental to ensuring the quality and safety of pharmaceutical products. For laboratory documentation, cGMP emphasizes:
*   **Documentation Systems:** The establishment and maintenance of comprehensive documentation systems that control all aspects of manufacturing, testing, and quality control [28].
*   **Record Keeping:** The accurate, complete, and contemporaneous recording of all data and activities. This includes raw data, calculations, test results, deviations, and investigations.
*   **Audit Trails:** For electronic systems, the requirement for secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails to record all changes and deletions of cGMP data [29].
*   **Data Integrity:** The overarching principle that all data must be reliable and accurate, ensuring that decisions are based on sound scientific evidence.

### 9.1 Purpose of Change Control

Any proposed changes to this SOP, or any other controlled laboratory document, must be managed through a formal change control procedure. The purpose of change control is to ensure that all changes are properly reviewed, approved, implemented, and documented, minimizing the risk of errors, maintaining data integrity, and ensuring continued compliance with regulatory requirements. This process applies to all changes, whether minor or major, including revisions to procedures, forms, or electronic systems [30].

### 9.2 Change Request Initiation

Any personnel identifying a need for a change to this SOP or related documents must initiate a Change Request (CR) form. The CR form must clearly describe the proposed change, the reason for the change, and its potential impact on current operations, data integrity, and regulatory compliance. The initiator must provide sufficient justification for the proposed change.

### 9.3 Review and Assessment

Once a CR is initiated, it must be reviewed by relevant stakeholders, including Laboratory Management, Quality Assurance, and other affected departments. The review process involves assessing the technical feasibility of the change, its impact on product quality, safety, and regulatory compliance, and the resources required for implementation. A risk assessment should be conducted to evaluate potential risks associated with the change [31].

### 9.4 Approval of Change

Following the review and assessment, the CR must be approved by authorized personnel, typically Laboratory Management and the Quality Assurance Department. Approval signifies that the proposed change is acceptable and can be implemented. If the change is rejected, the reasons for rejection must be documented.

### 9.5 Implementation of Change

Approved changes must be implemented according to a defined plan. This may include updating the SOP, retraining personnel, validating new processes or systems, and updating relevant forms or records. All implementation activities must be documented, including the effective date of the change.

### 9.6 Post-Implementation Review

After implementation, a post-implementation review may be conducted to verify the effectiveness of the change and to ensure that it has achieved its intended purpose without introducing new issues. Any unexpected outcomes or further adjustments required must be addressed through the change control process [32].
